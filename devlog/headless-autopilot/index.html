21 September 2026 · Godot 4

I fly my game headless to find invisible walls

TwinThrust is a physics rocket lander with 40 hand-built levels. Before any of them reaches a human, a robot flies it. That habit started as paranoia about collision geometry and ended up catching the worst bug in the project.

The problem with looking at a level

Every level in TwinThrust is built in code, boxes, bars, hazards, pads, placed by a builder function. The obvious way to check one is to instantiate the level content on its own and dump what it produced. I did that for months, and it is close to useless, for a reason that took me an embarrassingly long time to see.

The level content is not the level. The shared floor, the ceiling, the start pad and the end pad all live in the main game scene, not in the per-level builder. So a standalone dump shows you everything except the four things most likely to be in your way. I "fixed" the wrong ceiling more than once that way, reasoning confidently about geometry I could see, while the thing actually blocking the craft was in a scene I had not loaded.

The fix is blunt: don't inspect the level. Boot the real game and fly it.

The rig

Each test is a SceneTree script run headless against the real game.tscn. It sets the chapter and level the way the menu would, waits for the scene to come up, and then drives the craft, either by synthesising the same key input a player would produce, or by steering directly with a PD controller toward a list of waypoints.

Then it listens. The craft emits a crashed signal, and the useful trick is that the signal's reason string tells you which kind of death you got: 

craft.crashed.connect(func(reason):
    print("crashed at ", craft.global_position, " reason='", reason, "'")
    for body in craft.get_colliding_bodies():
        print("  hit: ", body.get_path(), " groups=", body.get_groups()))

An empty reason means a plain solid-body collision, you flew into something. A non-empty one means a hazard guard deliberately killed you. That one distinction turns "the rocket blew up somewhere" into "the rocket hit this specific node at this specific position", which is the whole ballgame.

A few things I got wrong building it, in case they save someone an afternoon:

  • Walking a frozen body proves nothing. If you freeze the rocket and teleport it along a route to test clearances, it generates no collision response, so crashed never fires and you have verified precisely nothing. Probe with direct_space_state.intersect_shape() at each step instead.
  • Teleporting keeps stale contacts. Move a frozen rigid body and it still holds its old contact list, so the first unfrozen frame reports a crash at a speed of zero. Respawn through a proper reset instead of assigning a position.
  • GDScript lambdas capture by value. Accumulate results into an array, not by writing to a captured local, or your scan will cheerfully report nothing at all.

What it found

The finale of Chapter 2 is an escape: a dragon wakes up, chases you up a shaft, and the people on the radio give your craft a 4× boost to outrun him. It had never felt right. The flames got big, the audio got loud, and the rocket climbed at exactly the speed it had been climbing at before, about 395 px/s, pinned there like a governor was fitted.

I had assumed, for about three weeks, that this was a tuning problem.

It was one line, in the wrong function. The level's orchestration ran from _process, the render frame, and in the boost state it did a read-modify-write of the craft's linear_velocity every frame to bleed off lateral drift: 

# in _process, the render frame
var v = craft.linear_velocity
v.x = lerp(v.x, 0.0, drift_bleed)   # keep y, kill sideways drift
craft.linear_velocity = v           # ← this is the bug

That write lands between physics ticks, and it overwrites the velocity the body accumulated during the step. The boost was applying force perfectly well. The render loop was throwing the result away, sixty times a second, while faithfully preserving the y component that the boost was trying to change.

I found it by bisection in the end, commenting the write out and re-flying. Boost terminal velocity went from 449 px/s to about 2,500.

If a body seems to ignore forces and sits at a suspiciously constant speed, suspect a script writing its velocity every frame. Log velocity against actual position delta per tick: when position advances further than velocity × dt, something outside the physics step is editing the body.

The knock-ons were the interesting part, because they show how one bug hides behind another. The boost was on a 15-second clock, and a boost that does not accelerate does not cover the distance it was budgeted for, so the clock ran out roughly 13,000 px below the top of the shaft. Everything scheduled for the open sky afterwards therefore happened inside the shaft: the dialogue, the run-out, and the landing pad, which spawned 250 m dead ahead of a climbing rocket. The player's reward for surviving the finale was to smash into the underside of their own landing pad.

I had been treating that as a separate bug. It was the same one.

Two rules that came out of it

Craft physics writes go in _physics_process, always. Other levels that only read state can stay on the render frame; anything that writes body state moved to the tick. As a bonus, the dragon's pursuit lerp became frame-rate independent, which I had not realised was also broken.

End things on geometry, not on a clock. The boost now cuts out 900 px above the top of the shaft, "the boost dies as the shaft does", with a generous timer left in only as a fallback for someone who dawdles. A clock encodes an assumption about speed. Geometry encodes the thing you actually meant.

Was it worth it?

The finale now has a test that boots the real scene and flies the entire sequence, burn, collect, awaken, chase, boost, run-out, pad, landing, in two different styles, one committed and one timid, and asserts that both end with the craft landed and not dead. It runs headless in a few seconds.

That is the part I would recommend to anyone building a physics game solo. Not the autopilot itself, which is a scrappy PD controller and a list of waypoints. The habit: make the thing play itself, and make it complain in specific terms. You cannot see an invisible wall by looking, and you will not reliably find it by playing, because you will unconsciously fly around it. A robot with no such instincts will drive straight into it and tell you the node path.

TwinThrust is free on Android, 40 hand-built levels, two thumbs, no autopilot for you.
